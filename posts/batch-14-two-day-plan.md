# Batch 14. Two-day posting plan (3/day) - built on Georgia's 3 winning hook FAMILIES

From the latest Georgia call: stop inventing new hooks every time. Build repeatable hook families and test
angles within each. The 3 families that are working as trial reels:

- **F1 - Contrarian expertise.** "The first thing I check/do isn't X." / "Most brands think X. Actually Y."
- **F2 - Competitive gap / FOMO.** "Your competitor is doing X. Here's why." / "You're doing X, they're doing Y."
- **F3 - Pattern recognition / authority.** "Every winning X does Y." / "I analysed X and noticed Y." / "The one thing every X has in common."

Plan: each day = one reel from each family, so we test all three families every day and compare angles across
the two days. To the caption standard: much longer, 5-point save-worthy listicles, a different caption per hook,
Fraser's voice, em-dash-free, HOOKS CTA. One thing on screen · no tails · trending audio (↑) · arresting/tight
first frame (camera-rig POV or fill-frame).

Track each in results-log by FAMILY (F1/F2/F3) so we learn which family + which angle wins.

## Reel 1.1 - F1 Contrarian expertise
**Angle:** "The first thing I [do] isn't X" (variation of the proven "first thing I check")

**On-screen hook:** "The first thing I fix in a losing ad isn't the creative."
**B-roll:** tight POV camera-rig monitor with an ad playing / scrubbing the timeline. Fill frame one.

**Caption:**

Save this before you scrap your next ad 👇

When an ad flops, everyone's instinct is to reshoot it. New footage, new actor, new edit. I don't touch any of that first. Here's the order I actually go in, and it's saved brands thousands in reshoots:

1. The first line. Nine times out of ten a "bad ad" is a good ad with a weak opening. Before I change a single frame, I rewrite the first line to open on the customer's problem. Same footage, new hook, completely different result.

2. Where the product shows up. If the product appears in the first 3 seconds, that's usually the leak. I push it back until after the problem lands. People need a reason to care before they see what you're selling.

3. The first frame itself. Not the words, the visual. A dull, wide, empty opening frame kills a great line. I swap it for something tight or slightly odd that makes the thumb hesitate. That half second is the whole game.

4. The one message. Losing ads almost always say three things. I cut it to one, the single strongest reason to buy, and let everything else go. Clarity beats completeness every time.

5. Only then, the creative. If the hook, the timing, the frame and the message are all right and it still loses, now it's a creative problem. Which is almost never where it actually was.

Most "failed" ads were one line away from working. Fix the open before you spend a penny reshooting.

## Reel 1.2 - F2 Competitive gap / FOMO
**Angle:** "Your competitor is doing X. Here's why." (volume gap)

**On-screen hook:** "Your competitor launched 40 ads last month. You launched 4."
**B-roll:** tight, scrolling an ad library / competitor's ads stacked on a bright screen. Movement.

**Caption:**

Save this before you plan next month's creative 👇

Go look up any brand beating you in the ad library right now. I'll wait. You'll almost always find the same thing: they're not smarter than you, they're just shipping far more ads than you are. Here's why volume quietly wins, and how to close the gap:

1. More shots on goal, more winners. You can't predict which ad will pop, nobody can. The brand testing 40 finds more winners than the brand testing 4, purely by maths. Volume isn't laziness, it's how you buy more chances to get lucky on purpose.

2. They kill losers faster. When you only have 4 ads, you cling to them and "optimise" a loser for weeks. With 40, a weak ad gets cut in days because there's always a better one right behind it. Volume makes you ruthless.

3. They learn 10x faster. Every ad is a question to the market. 40 ads a month is 40 answers about what your customer responds to. 4 is barely a signal. The volume brand knows its audience in a quarter of the time.

4. They always have the next winner ready. Winners fatigue and die. The brand shipping volume never has a gap, because the replacement is already live. The low-volume brand falls off a cliff every time its one good ad burns out.

5. They're not spending more to do it. This is the part people miss. It's the same budget, spread across more ideas, with the losers cut fast. More ads doesn't mean more spend. It means the same spend working harder.

You don't need a bigger budget to catch up. You need more angles, shipped faster, cut sooner. That's it.

## Reel 1.3 - F3 Pattern recognition / authority
**Angle:** "I analysed X and noticed Y."

**On-screen hook:** "I analysed 500 winning ads and noticed the same 5-second pattern."
**B-roll:** tight, flipping through printed ad frames / camera-rig POV on a monitor. Fill frame.

**Caption:**

Save this before you write your next hook 👇

I pulled 500 of the best-performing ads I could find across every niche and watched only the first 5 seconds of each. Format, length and budget were all over the place. But the opening followed the same pattern almost every time. Here it is, second by second:

1. Second 1: a problem, not a product. The first thing on screen named something the viewer already felt. A frustration, a fear, a thought they'd had that morning. Never the brand, never the product. The problem is the door in.

2. Second 2: proof it's for you. Right after the problem, a signal that said "yes, you specifically." A number, a niche, a scenario so specific the right person felt caught. "If you're spending £20k a month on ads and still guessing…"

3. Second 3: an open loop. A promise of an answer they didn't get yet. "Here's what nobody tells you." The loop is what carries them past the swipe point. Close it too early and they leave.

4. Seconds 4 to 5: the first micro-payoff. Not the full answer, a taste. One quick, genuinely useful thing that proved the rest was worth staying for. Give value fast or the loop snaps.

5. Only then: the product. Never in the first 5 seconds. The product showed up once the viewer was already leaning in, as the answer to the problem, not the opening line.

Problem, proof, loop, payoff, then product. That's the shape under almost every winner I watched. Steal the order.

## Reel 2.1 - F1 Contrarian expertise
**Angle:** "Most brands think X. Actually, Y."

**On-screen hook:** "Most brands think they have a targeting problem. They don't."
**B-roll:** tight, camera-rig POV on a dashboard / hands adjusting a monitor. Fill frame one.

**Caption:**

Save this before you touch your audience settings again 👇

Every week a brand tells me their ads stopped working and they need better targeting. Almost every week they're wrong. Meta's targeting is smarter than it's ever been. The problem moved. Here's what's actually going on, and what to fix instead:

1. The algorithm finds your buyer now. You don't need laser audiences anymore, the machine does that if you feed it a clear signal. Broad targeting plus a sharp creative beats narrow targeting plus a vague one. Your creative is the targeting now.

2. A weak hook confuses the algorithm. If nobody stops, Meta can't learn who your ad is for. The "targeting problem" is often just a hook that never gave the algorithm a signal to optimise toward. Fix the stop, and the delivery sorts itself.

3. You're blaming delivery for a message miss. If the right people are seeing it and not converting, that's not targeting, that's the message not landing. More precise audiences won't save a line that doesn't hit. Rewrite the line.

4. Your ad talks to everyone. A vague ad forces the algorithm to guess who it's for, so it shows it to everyone and converts no one. Name the exact person in the creative and you've done the targeting inside the ad itself.

5. The offer isn't audience-worthy. Sometimes the perfect person sees the perfect ad and still doesn't buy, because the offer's flat. That's the last thing to check, and it's never fixed in the audience tab.

Stop rebuilding audiences. Your best targeting lever is the first line of your ad. Fix that first.

## Reel 2.2 - F2 Competitive gap / FOMO
**Angle:** "Your competitor is doing X (AI). Here's how." (framed for operators, not AI-hype)

**On-screen hook:** "Your competitor is using AI to test 100 ads a week. Here's how."
**B-roll:** tight, camera-rig POV / screen showing many ad variations in a grid. Movement, fill frame.

**Caption:**

Save this before your next creative sprint 👇

The brands pulling ahead aren't using AI to make weird videos. They're using it to do the boring, expensive part of testing at 10x the speed, for a fraction of the cost. Here's the actual workflow, no hype:

1. AI writes the variations, you pick the angles. You decide the 5 real customer problems worth testing. AI turns each into 10 hook variations in minutes. You're still the strategist, it just removes the blank-page bottleneck that used to cap you at a handful of ideas.

2. They test hooks before spending on production. Instead of shooting 10 ads, they run 10 AI-written hooks over the same footage and see which line stops people. Only the winners get the full production budget. Cheap to learn, expensive only where it's earned.

3. They localise instantly. One winning ad becomes five languages or five audiences by end of day, not end of month. The AI handles the grunt work, the brand handles the judgement of what's worth scaling.

4. They repurpose one winner into ten. A single proven ad gets recut into new hooks, new first frames, new formats with AI doing the heavy lifting. One winner feeds a month of tests instead of dying after two weeks.

5. The human still owns the taste. This is the part hype merchants skip. AI gives you volume, not judgement. The brands winning with it are the ones who know a good hook from a bad one and use AI to produce more of the good. Volume without taste is just noise.

AI isn't the strategy. It's the thing that lets a small team test like a big one. Use it for volume, keep the judgement human.

## Reel 2.3 - F3 Pattern recognition / authority
**Angle:** "Every winning X I've seen does Y."

**On-screen hook:** "Every ad that scaled this year did the same boring thing."
**B-roll:** tight, camera-rig POV on a monitor with an ad playing / stacked winners on screen. Fill frame.

**Caption:**

Save this before you try to be clever with your next ad 👇

I've watched a lot of ads scale this year. The ones that got big weren't the clever ones. They were almost boring in how simple they were, and they all did the same 5 unglamorous things:

1. They said one clear thing. No twist, no cleverness for its own sake. One problem, one promise, said plainly. The "creative" ads that tried to be too smart mostly confused people and died. Clarity scaled, cleverness didn't.

2. They looked like content, not an ad. Rough, native, shot on a phone half the time. The polished cinematic ones often got scrolled because they screamed "advert" in the first frame. Boring-looking beat beautiful.

3. They repeated the message. The winner said the core idea more than once, at the start and again near the end. Brands worry about being repetitive. Viewers barely paying attention need it twice to catch it once.

4. They had an obvious next step. No clever ambiguity about what to do. A plain, direct call to action that a distracted person could follow without thinking. Simple instructions beat clever ones every time.

5. They were tested, not agonised over. The scaling ads weren't the ones someone perfected for three weeks. They were the ones that got shipped, proven quickly, then pushed hard. Boring consistency beat rare brilliance.

The lesson keeps repeating: winning ads aren't clever, they're clear. Stop trying to make art. Make the point.
