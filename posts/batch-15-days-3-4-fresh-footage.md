## Reel 3.1 - F3 Pattern/authority (the SAVE family) - NEW FOOTAGE
**Angle:** "I analysed X and noticed Y" + "the ugliest won" (Fraser signature: ugly ads win)
**On-screen hook:** "I studied 1,000 ads this year. The best performers were the ugliest."
**B-roll (FRESH):** tight on a laptop screen scrubbing through a deliberately rough, ugly ad / a wall of ad
screenshots. Fill frame one with the screen. Movement of the scrub bar.

**Caption:**

Save this before you polish your next ad 👇

I went through 1,000 ads this year, sorted by what actually scaled. The uncomfortable truth: the best performers looked worse than the ones that flopped. Here are the 5 "ugly" traits the winners kept sharing:

1. They looked filmed on a phone. Shaky, real, unpolished. The cinematic ones screamed "advert" in the first frame and got skipped. Rough looks like content, and content gets watched. Polish is a tax, not a feature.

2. They opened mid-sentence. No logo, no intro, no title card. Straight into a person talking about a problem, like you'd caught them halfway through a thought. The setup is what loses people. Cut it.

3. They had one flat, plain message. No clever wordplay, no double meaning. One thing, said simply, repeated. The "creative" ones were too smart for a distracted viewer and died. Plain beat clever.

4. The captions were the default font. Not branded, not designed. The scrappy on-screen text looked native to the platform, so the brain didn't flag it as an ad. Over-designed captions are a giveaway. Blend in.

5. They ended abruptly. No slow outro, no "link in bio" sung over music. The point landed and it stopped. Winners respect that attention is borrowed, so they give it back the second the job's done.

Stop making your ads prettier. Make them clearer, rougher, and more like the content people actually chose to watch.

## Reel 3.2 - F2 Competitive/FOMO (the FOLLOW family) - NEW FOOTAGE
**Angle:** "Your competitor is doing X. Here's why." (their worst beats your best)
**On-screen hook:** "Your competitor's worst ad is still beating your best one."
**B-roll (FRESH):** tight, two phones side by side / split screen of two ads playing on a monitor. Fill frame,
obvious comparison. Movement.

**Caption:**

Save this before you blame your budget 👇

Harsh one. Go pull up a competitor who's outspending you in the ad library. Their weakest ad is probably still outperforming your best. It's rarely talent or budget. It's these 5 things they do that you don't:

1. They test enough to have a "worst" ad. You're comparing your one hero ad to their reject pile. They ship so many that even their leftovers have been battle-tested. Volume gives them a floor you don't have.

2. They found their angle by accident. Nobody's smart enough to guess the winning message. They shipped 30 angles, one caught fire, and now they ride it. Your best is a guess. Their best was discovered.

3. They iterate on winners, not from scratch. Once one works, they make 10 versions of it. You start every ad from a blank page. They compound. You reset. That gap widens every month.

4. They cut losers without ego. A weak ad gets killed in days, not defended for a quarter. You nurse an underperformer because you spent money making it. They treat that money as already gone and move on.

5. They post consistently, not in bursts. A steady drip of new creative keeps the account learning. You go quiet for weeks then dump five ads at once. The algorithm rewards the steady feeder, not the binger.

You don't need their budget. You need their habits. Ship more, iterate on winners, cut losers fast.

## Reel 3.3 - F1 Contrarian (the BALANCED family) - NEW FOOTAGE
**Angle:** "The first thing I do isn't X" (the proven Day-1 F1 shape)
**On-screen hook:** "The first thing I do with a winning ad isn't scale it."
**B-roll (FRESH):** tight on a dashboard showing one ad spiking / hand on a trackpad zooming a graph. Fill
frame with the screen. Movement.

**Caption:**

Save this before you pour budget into your next winner 👇

Everyone's instinct when an ad pops is to crank the budget. That's how you kill a winner in a week. Here's the order I actually go in the moment an ad starts working:

1. I make 5 variations of it first. Before touching spend, I clone the winner into new hooks, new first frames, new openings. Now I've got a stable of the thing that works, not a single ad about to fatigue under pressure.

2. I check WHY it won. Was it the hook, the frame, the offer, the audience? I don't scale a black box. If I can't name the reason, I can't reproduce it, and I'm one fatigue cycle from losing everything.

3. I raise budget slowly. 20% every couple of days, not double overnight. Big jumps reset the learning and spook the delivery. The ad needs to grow into the spend, not get shoved into it.

4. I build the next one immediately. The best time to create the replacement is while the winner's still winning. That way there's never a gap when it fades, and there's always a fade coming.

5. Only then do I push it hard. Once there are variations, I know why it works, and the replacement's in the pipeline, I scale with confidence. Scaling is the last step, not the first reaction.

The fastest way to waste a winner is to scale it before you understand it. Slow down at the exact moment everyone speeds up.

## Reel 4.1 - F3 Pattern/authority (the SAVE family) - NEW FOOTAGE
**Angle:** "Every winning X does Y" + specific stakes ("million-pound ads")
**On-screen hook:** "Every million-pound ad I've made opened the same 3 ways."
**B-roll (FRESH):** tight on a notebook / hand writing hooks with a marker, or flipping printed ad frames on a
desk. Fill frame one. Movement of the pen / pages.

**Caption:**

Save this before you write your next opening line 👇

I've made ads that have done millions in sales. Different products, different niches, different budgets. But the opening move was almost always one of these 3. Steal all of them:

1. The mirror. Open by describing the viewer's exact situation so precisely they think you're spying. "You've got 40 ads in your account and no idea which one's actually working." Recognition stops the scroll harder than any clever line.

2. The enemy. Name a villain, but never a competitor. A feeling, a habit, a lie the industry tells. "More budget won't fix a bad hook." Now it's you and the viewer against a common enemy, and they're listening.

3. The forbidden truth. Open with something that sounds like you shouldn't say it out loud. "Most of your ads are losing money and your agency knows." A little danger buys attention that safe, polished openings never will.

Notice what none of them do: none open on the product. None open on the brand. None open on a feature. Every one opens inside the customer's head.

Pick one of these three before you write anything else. The rest of the ad is downstream of which door you open with.

**On-screen hook:** "While you perfected one ad, your competitor shipped 20."

**Caption:**

Save this before you spend another week on one ad 👇

The month you spent perfecting one ad, your competitor shipped 20 rough ones and found 3 winners. Speed beats polish in paid social, almost every time. Here's why the fast brand wins:

1. Done teaches you more than perfect. A shipped ad returns real data. A perfect ad still in the edit returns nothing. The fast brand has 20 lessons while you're still colour-grading lesson one.

2. The market moves before you finish. By the time your perfect ad is live, the angle's gone stale or a trend has shifted. Fast brands catch waves. Slow brands document them.

3. Perfectionism is fear in a nicer outfit. Endless tweaking is usually avoidance of the scary part, putting it out and being judged. The fast brand made peace with shipping B+ work, and B+ shipped beats A+ someday.

4. Winners come from volume, not agonising. You cannot think your way to the winning ad. You find it by shipping enough that one catches. 20 rough ads will out-find 1 polished one every single time.

5. Speed compounds. Every ad you ship makes the next one faster and better. The fast brand's 20th ad this month is sharper than your 1st, because they've had 19 reps and you've had none.

Lower the bar for shipping. Raise it for what you scale. That's the whole trick.

## Reel 4.3 - F1 Contrarian (the BALANCED family) - NEW FOOTAGE
**Angle:** "The first thing I check isn't X" (proven Day-1 F1 shape, new topic)
**On-screen hook:** "The first thing I check on a scaling ad isn't the ROAS."
**B-roll (FRESH):** tight on a metrics dashboard, hand scrolling past the ROAS column to hook rate / retention
graph. Fill frame with the screen. Movement.

**Caption:**

Save this before your next reporting call 👇

When an ad is scaling, everyone stares at ROAS. I barely look at it first. ROAS tells you what happened, not whether it'll last. Here's what I actually check, in order, to know if a winner has legs:

1. Hook rate. What percent get past 3 seconds? A scaling ad with a weak hook is running on borrowed time, it's winning on offer or luck, and it'll snap. Strong hook rate is what makes a winner durable.

2. Hold rate. Do people watch to the end, or bail at second 6? A high ROAS with a cliff in retention means a tiny hardcore audience is carrying it. That doesn't scale, it just looks good today.

3. Frequency. How many times is the same person seeing it? Climbing frequency with holding ROAS means fatigue is loading behind the scenes. The ROAS drop is coming, this is your early warning.

4. Comment sentiment. What are people actually saying? Real curiosity and questions mean it's got room to run. Silence or annoyance means the numbers are about to follow. The comments move before the CPA does.

5. THEN ROAS, in context. Now I read ROAS knowing whether it's built on a strong hook and healthy retention, or propped up by a fluke about to fade. Same number, completely different decision.

ROAS is the scoreboard, not the diagnosis. Check the leading indicators first, and you'll scale winners that last instead of chasing ones that were already dying.
