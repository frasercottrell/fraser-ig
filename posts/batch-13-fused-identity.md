## 1. "If you're paying an agency and still guessing what's working, this is why."

**On-screen hook:** "If you're paying an agency and still guessing what's working."
**B-roll:** tight POV camera-rig monitor with an ad playing, or scrubbing a dashboard on a bright screen. Fill frame one.

**Caption:**

Save this before your next agency call 👇

If you're paying a retainer and still can't answer "what's actually working this month," that's not on you. It's on the reporting. Here are the 5 things a good agency shows you every single week, without you having to ask:

1. Hook rate per ad. Not just spend and ROAS. The percent that make it past 3 seconds, ad by ad. If they can't tell you which hooks are stopping people, they don't know why anything worked, they're just watching the ROAS number move.

2. What they're testing, on purpose. A real test plan names the variable. "We're testing problem-first hooks against product-first this week." If the answer is a vague "we're optimising," there is no test, just spend.

3. Why the winner won. Any agency can find the winning ad. A good one tells you the reason it won, the hook, the angle, the first frame, so it can be repeated. If they can't explain it, they can't reproduce it.

4. The next three ideas in the pipeline. Winners die. If there's nothing queued behind the current best ad, your account is one fatigue cycle from falling off. You should always see what's coming next.

5. What they'd cut. An agency that only reports wins is hiding the losses. The honest ones show you what they killed this week and why. That's how you know they're actually managing the account, not babysitting it.

If you're not seeing these five, you're paying for activity, not results. Ask for them by name on your next call.

## 2. "Founders: you're too close to your brand to judge your own ads."

**On-screen hook:** "You're too close to your brand to judge your own ads."
**B-roll:** tight, hands holding a phone playing an ad / reacting to a monitor. Fill the frame.

**Caption:**

Save this before you approve your next batch 👇

I've watched hundreds of founders kill winning ads and greenlight losers, for the same reason every time: they judge ads as the person who built the product, not the stranger scrolling past. Here are the 5 ways it happens, and how to catch yourself:

1. You approve what makes you proud. The hero shot, the origin story, the feature you sweated over. None of it stops a stranger who doesn't care about you yet. Approve what stops the scroll, not what flatters the brand.

2. You reject the "ugly" ones. The rough, plain, unpolished ad often outperforms the cinematic one, because it looks like content, not an ad. If it feels too scrappy to you, that's often the reason it works.

3. You edit out the problem. Founders hate leading with the pain their product solves, it feels negative. But the problem is the hook. Cut it and you've cut the only line that makes the right person stop.

4. You add one more benefit. And one more. You know all of them, so you want them all in. The ad that lists five benefits lands none. Pick the single strongest and let the rest go.

5. You trust your gut over the data. Your gut knows the product. It doesn't know the customer. When your opinion and the numbers disagree, the numbers are the customer talking. Listen to them.

The fix isn't to stop having taste. It's to test past it. Your job is to run the experiment, not to be the judge.

## 3. "Ecommerce brands keep making this one expensive mistake."

**On-screen hook:** "Ecommerce brands keep making this expensive mistake."
**B-roll:** tight POV camera-rig monitor / product in hand on a bright, busy frame. Movement.

**Caption:**

Save this before you scale your next product 👇

Most ecommerce brands don't have a traffic problem. They have a message problem, and they pour ad budget on top of it hoping volume fixes it. It doesn't. Here are the 5 expensive mistakes I see on repeat:

1. Selling the product, not the outcome. Nobody wants a candle. They want the room to feel calm after a brutal day. Lead with the outcome the customer actually pays for, and let the product be how they get it.

2. Testing offers too slowly. Brands run one offer for a quarter and call it strategy. Your offer is your biggest lever. Test a new angle on it every couple of weeks, not twice a year.

3. Leaning on discounts to sell. If the only reason to buy today is 20% off, you've taught people to wait for the next sale. Build a reason to buy now that isn't price, urgency the product creates, not a code.

4. One creative, ten times. Ten edits of the same idea isn't testing, it's repeating. Test genuinely different angles, different problems, different customers, different hooks. You're hunting for the one that unlocks a new pocket of buyers.

5. Driving traffic to a boring offer. Great ads pointed at an unexciting offer just help people leave faster. If the ads work but nothing converts, the problem moved past the creative. Fix the offer before you spend more.

Traffic is not the growth lever most brands think it is. The message and the offer are. Fix those and the same spend does more.

## 4. "More ad spend isn't your problem."

**On-screen hook:** "More ad spend isn't your problem."
**B-roll:** tight, reviewing a spend dashboard on a bright screen / camera-rig POV. Fill frame one.

**Caption:**

Save this before you raise your budget 👇

Everyone's first instinct when ads plateau is to spend more. Nine times out of ten that just buys you the same result at a higher price. Before you touch the budget, fix these 5 things, in this order:

1. The hook rate. If under 30% of people make it past 3 seconds, more spend just shows a weak ad to more people. No budget fixes a hook that doesn't stop anyone. Start here, always.

2. The number of live angles. If everything is one concept in different clothes, you don't have a spend problem, you have an ideas problem. More budget on one tired angle burns faster, it doesn't scale.

3. The offer. If the ads are strong but sales are flat, the leak is downstream. Pouring more traffic into a weak offer just makes the leak bigger. Fix what happens after the click first.

4. Creative refresh rate. Winners fatigue. If you're not shipping fresh creative every week, adding budget just accelerates the burnout of the ad you're leaning on. Feed the machine before you feed it more money.

5. What you're actually measuring. If you're scaling on ROAS alone, you're flying half blind. Look at hook rate, hold rate, and cost per new customer. Spend more only once those are healthy, then it multiplies instead of leaks.

Budget is a multiplier. Multiply a broken funnel and you get a bigger broken funnel. Fix the five above and the same money works harder.

## 5. "This is why Apple's ads feel so different (and what you can steal)."

**On-screen hook:** "This is why Apple's ads feel so different."
**B-roll:** tight, an Apple ad playing on a screen / camera-rig POV on the monitor. Fill the frame.

**Caption:**

Save this before you write your next brand ad 👇

Apple spends a fortune, so it's easy to write off. But the reasons their ads feel different have nothing to do with budget, and you can steal all five today:

1. They sell the feeling, not the spec. Not "48 megapixel camera" but the look on a dad's face seeing the photo. Lead with what the product lets you feel, then let the spec back it up. People buy the feeling and justify with the number.

2. They show, they don't tell. No voiceover listing benefits. They put the product in a real moment and let you draw the conclusion. Trust the viewer to get it. Spelling it out kills the magic.

3. They keep one idea per ad. One feature, one feeling, one story. They could cram in ten things. They pick one and make you remember it. Restraint is the whole aesthetic.

4. They earn the logo. The Apple mark lands at the end, after you already feel something. The brand is the full stop, not the opening line. Yours should be too, stop opening on your logo.

5. They make the customer the hero. It's never "look how clever Apple is." It's "look what you can do." You're the one taking the photo, making the film, doing the thing. The product is just in your hand while you do it.

You don't have Apple's budget. You don't need it. Pick one feeling, show don't tell, and make the customer the hero. That's free.
